Section 9 — Close Assault
=========================

9.1  Declaration and Eligibility
--------------------------------


**9.1.1**  A unit may declare a close assault against an enemy unit in an adjacent hex. This costs 1 AP.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Sets the base cost and adjacency requirement, keeping close assault a considered, AP-priced choice rather than a free action — weapon and fire-line strength are intentionally left out of the declaration cost, since Rules 9.3–9.4 already gate that through the phases themselves.

   **Example:** Squad Alpha is adjacent to Squad Bravo's hex and has AP remaining this impulse. Declaring Close Assault costs Alpha 1 AP, the same as any other Regular action (Rule 6.3.2) — there is no separate "melee surcharge."

    *See also: Rule 18.9a (Close Assault Against a Vehicle) — when the target hex holds only enemy vehicles, this same declaration leads to a different resolution than Rules 9.3-9.4.*

**9.1.2**  The assaulting unit must have M# > 0, no FIRED or MOVED/FIRED marker present this turn, and a leader present in its hex to direct the assault — either coordinating that unit alone or activating it together with other units in the same stack (Rule 6.1.1). Closing with the enemy and physically entering a contested hex is a directed, coordinated action, never a rifleman's own spontaneous initiative, so this requirement applies no matter which of the two declaration paths below the unit uses. A unit with no leader present may still take any other action it's otherwise eligible for, but may not initiate a close assault.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Requires leader coordination for every close assault, fresh or ASSAULT-marked alike (see design note E.105, amending E.95's fresh-unit exception) — even a unit that hasn't done anything else yet this turn doesn't charge on its own initiative the instant it's adjacent to the enemy. The two-path split below is only about **which part-action** the assault costs, not about whether a leader is required at all.

   **Example:** Rifle Squad Alpha is completely fresh (no ASSAULT marker) and adjacent to Bravo's hex, with Sergeant Krebs (CMD 1) present in its hex. Alpha may declare Close Assault as its own Regular action. Had Krebs not been there, Alpha could still fire or move this turn like any other unit, but could not initiate the assault — no matter how fresh it was.

- **Fresh declaration**: a completely fresh unit (no ASSAULT marker) may declare Close Assault as its own Regular action (Rule 6.3.2).
- **ASSAULT-marked declaration**: a unit already carrying the ASSAULT marker (one part-action already spent this turn) may declare Close Assault as its **second** part-action only.

See Rule 6.3.3.

**9.1.3**  A Suppressed or Pinned unit may not declare a close assault.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** A Suppressed or Pinned unit is already struggling to function under fire; letting it launch a coordinated charge would contradict what those statuses represent — men keeping their heads down, not advancing.

   **Example:** Squad Alpha is currently Suppressed from a previous turn's fire. Even though it is otherwise eligible (fresh, adjacent to an enemy hex), Alpha cannot declare Close Assault until it recovers from Suppressed (Rule 5.2).

**9.1.3a**  A unit on its reduced (rear) face must pass a morale check before it may declare a close assault: roll 1d6 + Morale modifier against a threshold of 3 (Rule 15.2), with any leader CMD bonus in command radius applying as normal (Rule 15.2.4). A full-strength unit needs no such check. If the check fails, Close Assault is not declared and the unit's Action Point is not spent — the same outcome as an assault cancelled by Defensive Fire (Rule 9.1.5) — and the unit may act normally some other way this impulse.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** A full-strength squad going forward together still has the numbers and mutual confidence to close the last few metres; a unit that has already taken a step loss is asking fewer, more exposed men to do something far more dangerous than firing from cover — the same psychological gap Section 15 already models for every other stress trigger in the game, just applied at the moment of decision rather than after a result lands. Threshold 3 deliberately reuses the existing Suppressed recovery threshold (Rule 5.2) rather than introducing a new number to memorize — a Regular unit's odds here (67%) are already familiar from recovery rolls.

   **Example:** Alpha, on its reduced face after an earlier step loss, is adjacent to Bravo's hex with AP remaining. Before declaring Close Assault it rolls 1d6 + Morale modifier against threshold 3. A Regular unit's Morale modifier of +0 needs a 3 or better — a roll of 2 fails, and Alpha may not declare the assault this impulse, though its Action Point is untouched and it may act some other way instead. A fresh, full-strength unit in the same position could declare immediately with no check at all.

**9.1.4**  Close assault consists of up to three phases: Grenade Phase, Entry Fire Phase, and Melee Continuation (if both sides survive). Exception: a close assault declared against a hex containing only enemy vehicles resolves as a single exchange instead (Rule 18.9a) — none of these three phases apply.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Names the three-phase shape up front so the detailed phase rules that follow (9.3–9.5) read as an expansion of a known structure rather than a surprise partway through the section.

   **Example:** A single close assault might resolve entirely in the Grenade Phase (9.3.6/9.3.7), or continue through Entry Fire (9.4) and into Melee Continuation across several subsequent turns (9.5) if both sides keep surviving.

**9.1.5**  Defensive Fire: after the assault is declared, during the declaration window (Rule 5.5.2), the defending player may spend 1 RP on Defensive Fire (Rule 6.4) with the assaulted unit or any other friendly unit with LOS to the assaulting unit. Defensive fire resolves at the actual range (1 for the assaulted unit) with normal cover modifiers, before any assault phase. If it leaves the assaulting unit Suppressed or Pinned, the assault is cancelled per Rule 5.5.2 — the attacker retains the AP.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Gives the defender one chance to stop an assault before committing to the phases, but it costs the defender their own scarce RP rather than triggering automatically — the attacker's declaration still carries real risk for the defender to spend a resource against.

   **Example:** Alpha declares Close Assault on Bravo's hex. Before the Grenade Phase, Bravo's player spends 1 RP for Defensive Fire at range 1. The result leaves Alpha Suppressed — the assault is cancelled, but Alpha keeps the 1 AP it already spent to declare.

**9.1.6**  Throughout close assault, any result of Broken (margin 23+) inflicted during any phase is treated as **Dispersed** (Rule 10.5), not Broken: at grenade range men scatter out of the fight where a unit under ranged fire flees as a body. The counter goes to the DISPERSED zone and a serialised DISPERSED marker is placed in the hex.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** At grenade range, a unit hit hard enough to normally Break instead scatters as individuals rather than retreating as an organized body — a different physical reality from the stand-off ranged fire that Rule 10.4's Broken result describes.

   **Example:** During the Grenade Phase, Bravo suffers a margin-25 result. Under ordinary ranged-fire rules that would be Broken (Rule 10.4); in close assault it is instead Dispersed (Rule 10.5) — Bravo's counter goes to the DISPERSED zone with a serialised marker left in the hex, rather than to the BROKEN zone.

    *See also: Rule 10.5 (Dispersed), Rule 10.4 (Broken — ranged fire only).*

9.2  Voluntary Withdrawal
-------------------------


**9.2.1**  Before the Grenade Phase begins, the defending player may choose voluntary withdrawal.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Gives the defender a chance to avoid the fight altogether before the first shot is fired, reflecting a defender who sees the assault coming and chooses to fall back rather than stand and trade fire.

   **Example:** Alpha declares Close Assault on Bravo. Before grenades are thrown, Bravo's player checks whether voluntary withdrawal is available under 9.2.2.

**9.2.2**  Voluntary withdrawal is available only if: the defending unit is not Suppressed or Pinned; a clear withdrawal hex exists (not blocked, not occupied by enemy); and the defending unit is completely fresh this turn (no ASSAULT, FIRED, or MOVED/FIRED marker present). A unit that has already acted — even just to move — has already shown itself engaged and no longer qualifies for a bloodless withdrawal.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Only a unit that hasn't yet committed to the fight — hasn't fired, moved, or otherwise engaged — gets the option of a bloodless exit; a unit that already acted has shown itself and lost that chance.

   **Example:** Bravo fired earlier this turn, so it carries a FIRED marker, before Alpha's assault was declared. Even though Bravo is otherwise unSuppressed with a clear hex behind it, it no longer qualifies for voluntary withdrawal and must fight the Grenade Phase.

**9.2.3**  On voluntary withdrawal, the defender moves 1 hex away from the assaulting unit's approach direction. The attacker occupies the vacated hex unopposed. No combat occurs.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Withdrawal is bloodless specifically because it happens before combat — no dice are rolled, matching the idea that this is a tactical repositioning, not a fighting retreat.

   **Example:** Bravo withdraws 1 hex directly away from Alpha's approach. Alpha occupies Bravo's former hex without firing a shot; the assault ends with no casualties on either side.

9.3  Grenade Phase
------------------


**9.3.1**  If the defender does not withdraw, the Grenade Phase begins. Both sides throw grenades simultaneously.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** The Grenade Phase happens only once the defender has declined (or been unable to take) voluntary withdrawal — it's the fight's actual opening exchange, once both sides are committed to it.

   **Example:** Bravo did not withdraw (9.2.2 doesn't apply, or the defender simply chose to stand). The Grenade Phase begins: both players roll simultaneously per 9.3.2 and 9.3.3.

**9.3.2**  Attacker grenade attack: Roll 1d6+1d8+1d12 + attacker's G# value. Compare against defender's Defence + half the cover modifier (round down).

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** The three-die pool (d6+d8+d12) gives a wide, non-bell-curved spread appropriate to a short-range, close-quarters weapon — a grenade's outcome is dominated by luck (a good bounce, a dud) rather than skill, unlike aimed fire's tighter distributions.

   **Example:** Attacker has G3. Defender holds a building (cover +6, halved to +3 in the Grenade Phase per 9.3.5) with Defence 5. Attacker rolls 1d6=4, 1d8=5, 1d12=8 (17) + G3 = 20. Threshold = Defence 5 + half-cover 3 = 8. Margin = 20 − 8 = 12 → **Pinned** (9–13 band, Rule 8.6/Appendix C).

**9.3.2a**  If the assaulting unit's blind marker had not been spotted (Rule 14.9.6) at the moment it declared Close Assault, its Grenade Phase roll (9.3.2) gets **+3** — reusing the existing +3 figure (Rule 9.4.2's range-0 entry-fire bonus, Rule 14.10.2's Spot Action bonus) rather than inventing a new constant.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** A defender who never saw the assault coming gets no chance to brace for it — the same infiltration advantage night and concealment already model everywhere else in the hidden-information system, applied at the one moment concealment matters most. No new tracking is needed: this reads the hidden-info state (spotted or not) that already exists the instant Close Assault is declared.

   **Example:** A unit that closed the whole distance under cover of darkness without ever being spotted declares Close Assault and rolls its Grenade Phase attack (9.3.2) at +3 — the same bonus a Spot Action or a range-0 entry-fire shot already carries elsewhere in the rules. A unit that was spotted at any point before declaring gets no such bonus, regardless of how dark the scenario is.

    *See also: Rule 14.9.6 (the spot state this bonus reads), Rule 23.1 (why concealment is easier to keep at night in the first place).*

**9.3.3**  Defender grenade attack (simultaneous): Roll 1d6+1d8+1d12 + defender's G# value. Compare against attacker's Defence value. No cover modifier applies to the attacker — they are in the open approaching.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** The defender throws back under the same odds structure as the attacker, but the attacker gets no cover of their own — they're the exposed party, still closing the distance when the grenades land.

   **Example:** Bravo (G2) rolls 1d6+1d8+1d12 = 14, + G2 = 16, vs. Alpha's Defence 6 (no cover applies to Alpha in the open). Margin = 16 − 6 = 10 → **Pinned**.

**9.3.4**  Apply results to both sides simultaneously using the standard Result Threshold Table (Rule 8.6).

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Reuses the same results table already governing ranged fire instead of a separate close-assault-only table, keeping the whole system's outcome language consistent no matter which rule produced the margin.

   **Example:** Alpha's margin-12 Pinned result (9.3.2's example) and Bravo's margin-10 Pinned result (9.3.3's example) are both read straight off Appendix C — no separate close-assault threshold table exists.

**9.3.5**  Grenades halve the defender's cover modifier because they detonate inside the cover rather than against its exterior face.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Grenades explode inside the position a defender is using for cover, so that cover protects far less than it does against fire arriving from outside it.

   **Example:** A building's printed cover modifier is +6 against ranged fire. In the Grenade Phase, that halves (round down) to +3 for a defender inside it.

**9.3.6**  If the defender is eliminated (Dispersed, per Rule 9.1.6) in the Grenade Phase, the assault succeeds. The attacker enters the hex. The Entry Fire Phase does not occur.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Ends the assault immediately once the Grenade Phase alone has settled it, so Entry Fire never gets rolled against a hex that's already empty of defenders.

   **Example:** Bravo is Dispersed by the Grenade Phase. Alpha enters the hex immediately; the Entry Fire Phase (9.4) is skipped entirely.

**9.3.7**  If the attacker is eliminated in the Grenade Phase, the assault fails. The attacker is removed; the defender holds.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Mirrors 9.3.6 for the attacker's own elimination — a failed assault doesn't leave a phantom attacker around to fight on into a phase that no longer makes sense.

   **Example:** Alpha is eliminated in the Grenade Phase. Alpha's counter is removed from play; Bravo remains in its hex, undisturbed by any Entry Fire.

**9.3.8**  If both sides are eliminated simultaneously in the Grenade Phase, both counters are removed. The hex is empty.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Rules 9.3.6 and 9.3.7 each cover one side being wiped out alone, but the Grenade Phase is explicitly simultaneous (9.3.1) and can produce a mutually fatal roll on both sides at once — this closes that gap the same way Rule 9.4.7 already closes it one phase later for Entry Fire (that rule's own Why-note calls out "the specific case the grenade-phase rules don't address," which was this one). "Both sides" reads correctly whether each side is a single unit or a full stack (Rule 9.8) — the hex is emptied of combat units on whichever side(s) had every participant eliminated or Dispersed by the same phase's results.

   **Example:** Both Alpha and Bravo suffer margin-23+ results simultaneously in the Grenade Phase. Both counters are removed (Dispersed, per Rule 9.1.6); the hex is left empty, and the Entry Fire Phase never occurs.

    *See also: Rule 9.4.7 (the identical case for Entry Fire).*

9.4  Entry Fire Phase
---------------------


**9.4.1**  If both sides survive the Grenade Phase, the attacker enters the hex. Both sides fire simultaneously at range 0.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Entry fire happens at the worst possible range for both sides — point-blank — reflecting the moment the attacker physically steps into the contested hex.

   **Example:** Both Alpha and Bravo survived the Grenade Phase. Alpha now enters Bravo's hex; both sides fire simultaneously at range 0 per 9.4.2/9.4.3.

**9.4.2**  Attacker fires using their dominant fire line rFP + range 0 bonus (+3). A unit's **dominant fire line** is the fire line with the highest printed rFP (all lines are at full rFP at range 0; break ties in the owning player's favour). Only the dominant line fires in close assault — the confusion of a contested hex does not permit coordinated multi-weapon fire.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** A contested hex is too chaotic for a unit to coordinate multiple weapons at once, so only its single best line fires — this keeps a multi-weapon unit from getting the full-firepower advantage in melee that Rule 8's line combination allows at range.

   **Example:** Alpha has two printed fire lines, rFP 4 and rFP 6. Its dominant line (rFP 6) fires at range 0 with the +3 bonus for an effective rFP of 9; the rFP 4 line does not also fire.

**9.4.3**  Defender fires using their dominant fire line rFP + range 0 bonus (+3).

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** The defender's own dominant line gets the identical range-0 treatment as the attacker's, keeping the Entry Fire exchange symmetric — neither side is favoured for standing still versus advancing into the hex.

   **Example:** Bravo's dominant line is rFP 5. At range 0 with the bonus, Bravo fires at an effective rFP of 8.

**9.4.4**  No cover modifier applies in the Entry Fire Phase — both sides are in the same position.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Cover belongs to terrain, and both combatants are now standing in the exact same terrain — there's no longer an "inside" and "outside" of the position left to distinguish them.

   **Example:** Even though Bravo's hex is a building (normally +6 cover against ranged fire), no cover modifier applies to either side once Alpha has entered it for Entry Fire.

**9.4.5**  Apply results simultaneously using the standard Result Threshold Table.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Keeps a single, familiar results table doing all the outcome work across ranged fire, grenades, and now entry fire, rather than a bespoke melee-specific table.

   **Example:** Alpha's effective rFP 9 versus Bravo's Defence 5 (no cover) gives a margin of 4 → Suppressed, read off the same Appendix C table used everywhere else in the book.

**9.4.6**  If the defender is eliminated, the assault succeeds. If the attacker is eliminated, the assault fails and the attacker's counter is removed.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Entry Fire, unlike the Grenade Phase, is the last clean resolution point before a fight can drag on for multiple turns — its outcome decides whether the assault is already over.

   **Example:** Bravo is eliminated in Entry Fire. The assault succeeds and Alpha holds the hex. Had Alpha been eliminated instead, Alpha's counter would be removed and Bravo would hold.

**9.4.7**  If both sides are eliminated simultaneously, both counters are removed. The hex is empty.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Covers mutual destruction in Entry Fire specifically — Rule 9.3.8 covers the identical case one phase earlier, in the Grenade Phase.

   **Example:** Both Alpha and Bravo suffer Broken-equivalent results simultaneously in Entry Fire. Both counters are removed; the hex is left empty of combat units.

    *See also: Rule 9.3.8 (the identical case for the Grenade Phase).*

9.5  Melee Continuation
-----------------------


**9.5.1**  If both sides survive the Entry Fire Phase, the attacker remains in the hex. Melee continues into subsequent turns automatically — no AP is required to continue a melee already in progress.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Once both sides survive Entry Fire, the fight has become a standing melee rather than a single resolved action — it persists on its own, costing nothing further, matching a fight already underway rather than one either side chooses afresh each turn.

   **Example:** Alpha and Bravo both survive Entry Fire. Neither player spends any AP to keep fighting next turn — the melee simply continues into the following Recovery Phase.

**9.5.2**  At the start of each subsequent turn (during the Recovery Phase), units engaged in melee attempt recovery normally. Melee Continuation then resolves **once per turn, immediately after the Command Phase and before the first impulse of the Action Phase** — locked-in fighting is settled before either commander spends AP on anything else. No AP is required (Rule 9.5.1).

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Fixes melee resolution to one specific, predictable point in the turn sequence — right after Command Phase recovery, before any impulse — so an ongoing fight doesn't compete with or get skipped by whichever player wins initiative that turn.

   **Example:** At the start of turn 4, both sides' engaged units attempt recovery rolls first (Rule 5.2), then Melee Continuation resolves immediately afterward, before either player spends their first AP of the Action Phase.

**9.5.3**  During Melee Continuation turns, both sides fire simultaneously at range 0 with no cover modifier. Apply the range 0 bonus (+3 rFP) to both sides.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Reuses the exact Entry Fire conditions — range 0, no cover, +3 bonus — every subsequent turn, since nothing about the physical situation (two units locked together in the same hex) has changed.

   **Example:** On a second melee turn, Alpha and Bravo fire again at their dominant-line effective rFPs (9 and 8 respectively, as in the Entry Fire example), unaffected by any terrain.

**9.5.4**  Melee continues until one side is eliminated, one side fails a morale check and withdraws, or one side voluntarily withdraws at the start of a turn (if eligible per Rule 9.2).

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Names all three ways a melee actually ends, so no table needs to separately spell out "and it eventually stops" for every case that could produce that outcome.

   **Example:** The melee at Bravo's former hex ends only once one side is eliminated, one side fails a melee morale check and withdraws (9.6–9.7), or the defender voluntarily withdraws at a turn's start — though by this point most engaged units no longer qualify under 9.2's freshness requirement.

9.6  Melee Morale Check
-----------------------


**9.6.1**  After any melee turn in which a unit takes a Casualty result or worse, the affected unit must pass a morale check or attempt to withdraw. This melee morale check **replaces** the Section 15.1 morale check for results suffered in melee — a unit never checks twice for the same result.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Melee carries its own morale pressure — accumulating casualties while trapped in a hex is a different experience from taking one hit from long range — so it replaces rather than stacks with the ordinary ranged-fire morale check, keeping a unit from checking twice over the same wound.

   **Example:** A unit takes a Casualty result during a melee turn. It checks morale under Rule 9.6.2, not Rule 15.1 — the two never both apply to the same result.

**9.6.2**  Morale check: Roll 1d6 + Morale modifier (Rule 15.2.1a) vs. the break threshold for the result suffered.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Reuses the same Morale modifier used everywhere else in the book (15.2.1a) so melee doesn't need its own separate quality scale — only its own thresholds (the table below), tuned for the higher stakes of being trapped in a hex with the enemy.

   **Example:** A Regular unit (Morale modifier +0) suffers a Casualty result in melee. It rolls 1d6 + 0 against the break threshold of 3 (from the table below). A roll of 3 or higher holds; 1–2 fails.

.. list-table::
   :header-rows: 1
   :widths: auto

   * - **Result Suffered**
     - **Break Threshold**
   * - Casualty
     - 3
   * - Casualty + Suppressed
     - 4
   * - Broken (last unit of side eliminated)
     - N/A — side eliminated


**9.6.3**  If the roll meets or exceeds the break threshold, the unit holds. Melee continues.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** A passed check means the unit's nerve holds despite the casualty — the fight simply continues without interruption.

   **Example:** The unit from 9.6.2's example rolls a 4, meeting the threshold of 3. It holds, and melee continues into the next phase or turn.

**9.6.4**  If the roll is below the break threshold, the unit attempts withdrawal (see Rule 9.7).

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** A failed check is the trigger, not the resolution — what actually happens to a unit routing out of a melee hex (surrounded, constrained) is different enough from open-field routing that it gets its own procedure (9.7) rather than reusing Section 10's routing rules.

   **Example:** The unit from 9.6.2's example instead rolls a 2, below the threshold of 3. It does not simply rout in the open — it attempts the specific melee withdrawal procedure in Rule 9.7.

9.7  Withdrawal and Routing
---------------------------


**9.7.1**  A unit that fails a melee morale check attempts to withdraw from the melee hex.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Names the trigger explicitly so Rule 9.7's specific withdrawal procedure is clearly scoped to melee morale failures only, not ordinary battlefield routing.

   **Example:** Following its failed morale check (9.6.4), the unit now attempts to withdraw from the melee hex under 9.7.2/9.7.3.

**9.7.2**  The withdrawing unit moves 1 hex away from the melee hex in a direction away from the enemy. Place SUPPRESSED marker on the withdrawing unit.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Withdrawal from melee always carries the same Suppressed penalty, since disengaging from point-blank combat is inherently costly even when it succeeds — win or lose, a unit doesn't break contact clean.

   **Example:** The withdrawing unit moves 1 hex away from the melee hex, in a direction away from the enemy, and a SUPPRESSED marker is placed on it even though it escaped.

**9.7.3**  If no clear withdrawal hex is available (blocked terrain, enemy units in all adjacent hexes), the withdrawing unit is Dispersed instead of withdrawing. The counter moves to the DISPERSED zone of the Casualty Track in its current state. A serialised DISPERSED marker is placed in the current hex.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** A unit that's surrounded or boxed in by terrain has nowhere to withdraw to, so the same fate that applies elsewhere in close assault (Dispersed, per Rule 9.1.6) applies here too, rather than leaving a failed withdrawal undefined.

   **Example:** The withdrawing unit's only adjacent hexes are enemy-occupied or blocked terrain. Instead of withdrawing, it is Dispersed: the counter goes to the DISPERSED zone in its current state, with a serialised marker left in the hex.

**9.7.4**  After a successful withdrawal, the melee ends. The opposing unit occupies the hex.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** A successful withdrawal is a clean end to that unit's part in the melee — the vacated hex simply becomes the opponent's, with no further resolution needed.

   **Example:** The withdrawing unit successfully moves clear. The melee at that hex ends; Bravo, the remaining unit, now occupies it.

9.8  Multi-Unit Close Assault
-----------------------------


**9.8.1**  A Close Assault action activates one unit or one stack from a single hex (Rule 6.1.1). Every combat unit in the assaulting stack participates. Assaults converging from different hexes are separate Close Assault actions in separate impulses, each resolved in full against the current state of the defending hex.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** A whole stack assaulting together is still just one action from one hex — this keeps close assault's AP cost from scaling with how many units pile into the charge, while still making every combat unit in the stack actually fight.

   **Example:** A stack of two rifle squads in the same hex declares one Close Assault action (1 AP total) against an adjacent enemy hex. Both squads participate in every phase.

**9.8.2**  When either side has multiple participating combat units, that side makes **one** attack per phase using the **sum** of its participants' G# values (Grenade Phase) or the sum of their dominant fire line rFPs (Entry Fire and Melee Continuation, +3 range 0 bonus applied once).

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Multiple attackers don't each get a separate attack roll — their strength is pooled into one combined attack per phase, keeping stack combat resolvable with one roll instead of compounding accuracy across repeated rolls.

   **Example:** Two squads (G3 and G2) assault together. In the Grenade Phase they make one combined attack using G3+G2=G5, not two separate G3 and G2 attacks.

**9.8.3**  Each side's attack resolves against the **highest** Defence value among the opposing side's participating units — a stack defends as well as its best-positioned element. Cover handling is unchanged (halved in the Grenade Phase, none thereafter).

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** A stack's defence is only as good as its best-positioned member — attackers can't simply target a stack's weakest unit on paper, since one well-placed defender can anchor the whole stack's Defence value.

   **Example:** A defending stack has one unit with Defence 5 and one with Defence 7. The attacker's combined attack resolves against Defence 7, the higher of the two, not an average or the lower value.

**9.8.4**  The receiving player allocates each result to one of their participating combat units. No unit may be allocated a second result in the same phase until every participating unit has been allocated one.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Spreads incoming results across a stack's units one at a time rather than letting the receiving player stack every result onto a single expendable unit, which would let a bad roll's severity be dodged by sacrificing one unit repeatedly.

   **Example:** A two-unit stack takes two results in the same phase. The receiving player must assign the first to one unit and the second to the other — neither unit may take both while the other has none.

**9.8.5**  Melee morale checks (Rule 9.6) and withdrawals (Rules 9.2, 9.7) are per unit. A side remains in the melee while it has at least one combat unit in the hex; units that withdraw or disperse leave individually.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Keeps morale and withdrawal personal to each unit even inside a shared melee, since one unit's nerve breaking doesn't mean its stack-mate's does too — the fight only actually ends once every combat unit on a side has left or been eliminated.

   **Example:** One unit in a two-unit stack fails its melee morale check and withdraws (9.7). The other unit, having passed its own check, remains and continues the melee alone.

**9.8.6**  Leaders and weapon counters in a melee hex do not fight and are never allocated results while a friendly combat unit remains. If their side's last combat unit is eliminated or leaves the hex, they share the hex's fate: a leader or weapon counter left alone with the enemy is captured (Rule 11.2a); one whose side withdrew may withdraw with any withdrawing unit.

.. container:: rule-guide

   **Why:** Leaders and support weapons contribute nothing to a melee's fighting power, so they're kept out of harm's way as long as a combat unit is still there to draw fire — but once that protection is gone, they share whatever fate befalls the hex, since there's no longer a fighting unit's stack presence to hide behind.

   **Example:** A leader is stacked with a combat unit that gets eliminated in melee, leaving the leader alone with the enemy in the hex. The leader is administratively captured (Rule 11.2a) rather than continuing to fight.

    *See also: Rule 11.2a (Administrative Capture), Rule 3.5.1 (stacking limit — 3 combat units).*
