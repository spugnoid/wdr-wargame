# US 57mm Anti-Tank Gun, M1 — Ballistics-Reuse Question, Crew, 1943 Organizational Context, and Deployment Notes

Research pass for this project's next towed-AT-gun candidate under Rule 17.1a, following
`counters/toe/pak40_1943.md` (first towed gun, German PaK 40) and `counters/toe/sixpdr_1943.md`
(second, British 6pdr). Unlike those two files, **ballistics are not already settled here** —
the task brief specifically asked whether the existing `sixpdr_57l50_apcbc` row in
`counters/armor_calc/data/guns.csv` (MV 2730 fps / 831 m/s, `capped` ammo family, fitted from
Bird & Livingston 2001 calibration points, see `counters/toe/british_vehicles_1943.md` and
design note E.118) can simply be reused for the US 57mm Gun M1, since the M1 is a licence-built
near-copy of the same British Ordnance QF 6-pounder. That question is answered first, in detail,
before the usual crew/organizational/TOE sections.

## Ballistics: Can `sixpdr_57l50_apcbc` Be Reused Directly?

**Short answer: not cleanly for a 1943-dated entry — this is genuinely closer to the PaK 40's
own APCBC-vs-APCR situation (same gun, different fitted curve per ammunition nature) than to
the StuG III/Panzer IV KwK40 situation (literally the identical gun and ammunition, reuse the
curve outright).** The reasoning:

**What is identical:** the barrel. Wikipedia's wikitext is explicit that "since there was
sufficient lathe capacity, the longer barrel could be produced from the start" (Zaloga 2005
p.13) — i.e. the M1 was built on the 6-pounder Mk II pattern but with the longer **L/50** barrel
from day one, the same barrel length already used to fit `sixpdr_57l50_apcbc`. There is no
"L/43 M1" to worry about the way there is an L/43 6pdr Mk II/III. Muzzle energy delivered to a
given projectile by the barrel itself should therefore be essentially the same as the British
L/50 gun's.

**What is not identical: the ammunition actually issued in 1943.** The full ammunition table
(transcribed directly from the article's wikitext, "L/50 guns" column):

| Nature | Model | Weight | Filler | MV (L/50) | In guns.csv already? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| British APCBC (from Jan 1943) | Shot, APCBC, Mk 9T | 3.23 kg | — | 831 m/s (2730 fps) | Yes — `sixpdr_57l50_apcbc` |
| British APC (from Sept 1942) | Shot, APC, Mk 8T | 2.86 kg | — | 884 m/s (2900 fps) | No |
| British AP (uncapped) | Shot, AP, Mks 1-7 | 2.86 kg | — | 892 m/s (2930 fps) | No |
| **US AP** | **AP Shot M70** | **2.85 kg** | **none (solid steel)** | **853 m/s (2800 fps)** | **No** |
| US APC/APHE | APC Shell M86 (Wikipedia) / "M85" (Tank Archives) | 3.30 kg | Dunnite, 34-42 g | 823 m/s (2700 fps) | No |

The critical, sourced fact for a **1943** roster entry specifically: **the US M1 in 1943 fired
only the uncapped M70 AP shot.** Wikipedia states this plainly, citing Zaloga p.16: "Because of
the unexpected adoption into service, the only ammunition type in production in the US was AP
ammunition" — i.e. the capped M86/M85 shell was not yet available when the gun entered combat.
Tank Archives corroborates the timing from the other direction: "Only AP ammunition was produced
until the end of the summer of 1944," naming M70 and M85 as that era's whole AP-category
inventory, with HE (M303) not arriving until "late summer of 1944." Both sources agree: whatever
capped-shot nature the M1 eventually got, it was not what was in the guns in Sicily or Italy in
1943.

This matters for more than just the muzzle-velocity number. **M70 is a genuinely different
ammunition nature from the British APCBC round already in guns.csv**, not just a faster/slower
version of the same thing:

- **M70 (2800 fps)** — uncapped monobloc AP shot, no armour-piercing cap, no ballistic cap, no
  filler. This project's existing `ammo_nature`/`ammo_family` scheme has no exact analogue yet:
  every other US/German/Soviet gun already in `guns.csv` uses the `capped` family (APC/APCBC),
  and the one genuinely uncapped-shot-adjacent row that exists, `t70_45l46_apbc`, is `apbc`
  (ballistic-capped, not armour-piercing-capped, and a much smaller 45mm projectile besides) —
  not a clean stand-in either.
- **British APCBC Mk 9T (2730 fps, already fitted)** — has both an armour-piercing cap and a
  ballistic cap, `capped` family, and was itself only introduced from January 1943 — meaning it
  postdates the M70/uncapped-shot design lineage entirely; it is not simply "the same round the
  US used, under a different label."

A capped nature — armour-piercing or ballistic — measurably changes penetration behaviour at
oblique impact and against face-hardened plate relative to plain AP shot; this project's own
fitted curves already treat ammo nature (not just calibre/barrel) as the thing that changes a
gun's K-factor and effective exponent (see the PaK 40's own two separate rows, `_apcbc` and
`_apcr`, same gun and barrel, different fitted curves, in `guns.csv`). Reusing the sixpdr's
capped-APCBC curve for a gun that in 1943 fired an uncapped shot would silently misrepresent
that difference, in the same direction the PaK40/APCR row exists specifically to avoid.

**A second, independent data point supporting "needs its own curve," found this session:** Tank
Archives gives a full penetration-vs-range table for the M70 shot specifically, "at 90 degrees"
(this appears to be the Soviet-testing-report convention for perpendicular/0°-obliquity impact,
i.e. the same convention this project's own 0°-obliquity Bird & Livingston table for the 6pdr
uses — **not independently confirmed which convention this specific source means**, flagged as
an open question below, not assumed): 135mm at 100m, 112mm at 500m, 89mm at 1000m, 70mm at
1500m, 55mm at 2000m. This is a real, citable, 5-point calibration-shaped dataset for exactly
the ammunition nature (M70) that a 1943 US roster entry would actually need — genuinely usable
as a seed for a future from-scratch fit — but **fitting that curve was out of this research
task's scope** (the brief asked only whether reuse is defensible, not to build a replacement),
and it has not been added to `gun_calibration.csv` or `guns.csv` this session. Doing so is
flagged as the natural next step if the designer wants this gun fielded with its own curve
rather than borrowing.

**What would make reuse more defensible:** a **later-war** (1944+) US M1 roster entry, once the
M86/M85 capped shell had actually reached troops, would be a much closer ballistics match to the
British APCBC row already in `guns.csv` (823 m/s vs 831 m/s, both capped shot, under 1% apart —
genuinely comparable to how this project already treats near-identical guns as interchangeable).
That is not the entry this task was scoped to build, but worth recording for whenever a 1944 US
57mm entry is wanted.

One more loose end, flagged rather than resolved: **Wikipedia names the capped US round "APC
Shell M86"; Tank Archives names the same-spec round (3.3 kg, 823 m/s, ballistic cap, ~40g
explosive filler) "M85."** This looks like a simple typo/transcription slip in one source or the
other (both describe one weapon), but which is correct was not resolved this session — noted so
a future pass doesn't treat it as two different shells.

## Crew Size

**Genuinely three different figures were found this session, more spread than either the PaK40
or 6pdr files found for their own guns** — presented honestly rather than picked:

- **10 men**, per the home-nation primary source, **FM 23-75, 57-mm Gun M1** (1944): squad
  leader, gunner (#1), four cannoneers (#2-5), three ammunition bearers (#6-8), and a driver
  (#9). This is the same breakdown already quoted (secondhand, as "the wrong nation's figure")
  in `sixpdr_1943.md` — here it is the directly on-topic source. Per the manual, the gunner
  aimed/fired and worked closely with #2 (the loader); #3 passed ammunition to #2; #4 and #5
  prepared ammunition and also served as spotters/guards against tanks or aircraft; #6-8 were
  ammunition bearers; the driver doubled as a fourth ammunition bearer when not needed with the
  prime mover. Weapon issue: squad leader and driver — M1 Garand; gunner and cannoneers — M1911
  pistols; ammunition bearers — M1 carbines; the driver additionally carried an M7 grenade
  launcher, and **the squad as a whole was also issued a bazooka** (a second, close-range AT
  weapon organic to the same 10-man unit, alongside the gun itself).
- **6 men**, read out of that same FM 23-75 breakdown rather than a separate source: the
  gunner + four cannoneers (5) actually serving the piece, plus the squad leader in direct
  command of them, = 6 — which would line up with the converging "6-man gun-serving crew"
  figure this project already used for both the PaK 40 and the 6pdr, with the 3 ammunition
  bearers + driver as the additional detachment complement on top (4 more, matching the same
  "gun-serving crew vs full detachment" split the 6pdr file already flagged for the British/US
  6pdr-family guns generally). **This "6" reading is this document's own inference from the
  primary source's role list, not a number any source states directly** — presented as a
  plausible cross-check against the other two guns' figures, not as an independently sourced
  fact.
- **5 men**, per Tank Archives, stated as a direct comparison to the smaller 37mm M3 gun it
  replaced: "The crew was the same as for the M3 gun, five men (commander, gunner, loader, two
  ammunition carriers)." This is a **different, smaller** headcount than either figure above,
  from a translated-Soviet-testing-report-derived secondary source rather than a US Army
  primary manual, and it does not map cleanly onto FM 23-75's own role list (it names only one
  ammunition carrier role position beyond gunner/loader/commander, where FM 23-75 lists three
  ammunition bearers plus a driver). **Not reconciled with the 10/6 figures above — presented
  as a real, citable, but conflicting alternative**, exactly this project's stated preference
  for flagging genuine source disagreement over silently picking one number.

## 1943 Organizational Context

Directly sourced and specifically dated, from two independent lines that substantially agree:

- Per the **Table of Organization and Equipment (TO&E) of 15 July 1943** (Wikipedia, citing
  Zaloga 2005 p.16): the infantry division headquarters company's own "defense platoon" held
  **3** 57mm guns; each of the division's **three infantry regiments' Anti-tank Companies** held
  **9 guns (three platoons of three)**; and each **infantry battalion** had its own **platoon of
  3 guns**. Total: **57 fifty-seven-millimeter guns per division.**
- Tank Archives independently gives a near-identical picture, dated the M1's actual **TO&E
  introduction to 26 May 1943**: "Each regiment's anti-tank gun company had 9 M1 guns instead
  of 12 37mm M3 guns, arranged into three platoons of three guns each. The company also had a
  mine platoon with a bazooka in the HQ section. 1.5-ton Dodge WC62 or WC63 trucks were used as
  tractors. The infantry battalion had an anti-tank platoon in its HQ, consisting of three M1
  guns... Instead of 24 M3 guns, each regiment now had 18 M1 guns, and each division had 54
  guns." (54 vs. Wikipedia's 57 is not a real conflict — Tank Archives' 54 is the 3-regiments
  total only; adding the division HQ company's own 3-gun defense platoon, which Tank Archives
  doesn't itemize in this passage, reaches the same 57.)
- **The replacement of the 37mm gun M3 is real, dated, and specifically a spring-1943 event,
  not an ongoing 1943-wide transition that was already complete by the time this project's
  1943 window opens.** Both sources agree the M1 was "made standard issue in the spring of
  1943" (Wikipedia/Zaloga p.15) "following the experience of the North African Campaign"
  (Wikipedia) — i.e. the switch was a direct, sourced reaction to encountering German tanks in
  Tunisia. Per Tank Archives, the M1 formally entered the TO&E on **26 May 1943**, each
  regimental AT Company converting from 12× 37mm to 9× M1 (fewer guns, each individually more
  capable) and each division from 72 to 57(or 54, per the discrepancy above) 57mm guns overall.
- **Critically, the 37mm was not instantly or fully replaced — the two guns coexisted through
  Sicily and into Italy.** Tank Archives states plainly: "The guns made their debut in 1943,
  during the Sicilian campaign. Since the 37mm guns had not yet been entirely replaced, the
  Americans could compare how the two guns fared in combat... The 57mm guns had not forced out
  the 37mm guns entirely in Italy, but the M1 was the most numerous. 37mm guns in this region
  were only preserved above authorized strength." A photograph cited in the same source (34th
  Infantry Division, 4 July 1943, Rabat, Morocco — just before the Sicily landings) shows M1
  guns still being towed by improvised M3 half-tracks because the intended Dodge tow trucks
  had not yet arrived in-theater. **A 1943-dated US roster entry using the M1 is well-supported,
  but should not be read as implying the 37mm had vanished from the same regiments by that
  date** — both guns were genuinely present side by side for most of calendar 1943.
- **Towing vehicle: not the 3/4-ton truck.** Both Wikipedia and Tank Archives independently and
  specifically state the M1's tow vehicle was the heavier **Dodge WC-62/WC-63, a 1½-ton 6×6
  truck**, issued "in place of the 3/4 ton truck used with its predecessor, the 37mm gun"
  (Wikipedia, citing Zaloga p.16) — the M2 half-track is also named by Wikipedia as an
  alternative tractor. **This directly corrects the task brief's own working assumption** that
  the M1 was "typically towed by a 3/4-ton truck" — that description fits the 37mm it replaced,
  not the M1 itself, per both sources found this session.
- **A separate, tank-destroyer-battalion-context 57mm/76mm picture also existed in Italy from
  October 1943**, distinct from the regimental Anti-tank Company covered above: Tank Archives
  notes "the first 76.2mm M5 anti-tank guns weighing 2 tons appeared in Italy in October of
  1943... the M5 wasn't used by infantry divisions, but separate anti-tank [tank destroyer]
  battalions" — i.e. a second, heavier, non-infantry-organic AT tier began appearing in-theater
  in the same calendar year, parallel to (not instead of) the infantry regiment's own 57mm
  Anti-tank Company. Not the subject of this file, but flagged for completeness the same way
  the 6pdr file flagged the RA Anti-Tank Regiment as a second real 1943 tier for the British gun.

**Net read:** a 1943-dated US 57mm M1 roster entry is squarely correct for the regimental
Anti-tank Company (9 guns/company, 3 platoons of 3) and/or the infantry battalion's own 3-gun
AT platoon — both real, both dated to the same 15 July / 26 May 1943 TO&E, both organic-to-
infantry contexts exactly analogous to the 6pdr's own "battalion platoon + higher echelon"
both/and structure. Unlike the 6pdr file, though, there is no ambiguity about *which* gun this
tier used in 1943 (only the M1 — the 37mm's residual presence is a parallel-unit fact, not a
same-unit-different-gun ambiguity).

## Historical Validation Data

**No dramatic single-action "gun defeats tank(s)" anecdote parallel to the 6pdr's Robaa Valley
find was located for the US 57mm M1 in 1943**, despite specifically checking Wikipedia's
"Battle of Salerno," "36th Infantry Division (United States)," and "Battle of Gela (1943)"
articles for exactly that. None of the three mentions American 57mm or 37mm anti-tank guns at
all in the text retrieved this session — stated plainly as a real gap, not glossed over.

**What was found and is recommended instead is real, dated, and directly sourced, if less
dramatic: the M1's combat debut and the Sicily doctrine comparison, July-August 1943.**

- Per Tank Archives (citing the same Zaloga 2005 source line as the organizational facts
  above): "The guns made their debut in 1943, during the Sicilian campaign. Since the 37mm guns
  had not yet been entirely replaced, the Americans could compare how the two guns fared in
  combat. The results were mixed."
- A specific, named, real quote from **General George S. Patton** is given, comparing the two
  guns' battlefield mobility (not their killing power): "If a more powerful round can be
  produced for the 37mm gun, then it would be preferable to the 57mm gun on the offensive. It
  can be towed by a low 1/4 ton truck, while the 57mm gun needs a halftrack or a 3/4 ton truck
  with a high silhouette. Unlike the 37mm gun, the 57mm gun can't be moved by the crew off-road."
  This is a genuinely citable, dated (Sicily, 1943), named-officer anecdote — but it is a
  doctrine/logistics complaint about the gun's weight and towing signature, not a combat
  vignette of the gun destroying enemy armor.
- The doctrinal follow-up to this same Italian-campaign experience is dated just outside this
  project's 1943 window but is worth noting for context: **Field Manual FM 7-35, "Infantry:
  Antitank Company, Infantry Regiment and Antitank Platoon, Infantry Battalion,"** issued 15
  March 1944 "based on the results of combat in Italy" (Tank Archives), prescribed opening fire
  on tanks with the M1 at **750 meters**, advised siting guns to eliminate dead ground a halted
  tank might use, and noted enemy tanks rarely fired on the move. This is 1944-dated doctrine
  reflecting 1943 Italian-campaign lessons-learned, not itself a 1943 primary event.
- **Explicitly flagging, per the task brief's own framing:** the US 57mm M1's most famous
  combat actions (its Bastogne, December 1944 defense, the engagement most wargamers associate
  with this gun) are outside this project's 1943 scope entirely, and were not researched here
  for that reason.

**Confidence assessment:** the Sicily-debut/Patton-comparison material is well-corroborated (it
appears consistently in the one detailed source found, Tank Archives, itself tracing to the
named Zaloga 2005 monograph) but was not independently cross-checked against a second source
this session, unlike the 6pdr's Robaa Valley anecdote (three independent sites). Treat as
single-source, moderate-confidence, real and dated, but thinner than the 6pdr's own recommended
anecdote.

## Deployment/Limbering Notes for Rule 17.1a

Several real, sourced peculiarities worth flagging for whoever builds this roster entry:

- **Heavier/higher-silhouette tow requirement than the gun it replaced.** The M1 needed a
  1½-ton 6×6 truck (Dodge WC-62/63) or a half-track, not the 37mm's 1/4-ton jeep — Patton's own
  1943 complaint (above) specifically calls out the higher silhouette this created relative to
  the 37mm. If this project ever wants to differentiate towed-AT-gun counters by how
  conspicuous/vulnerable their prime mover is while limbered, this is a real, sourced basis for
  treating the US 57mm as less discreet in that state than a 37mm-class gun would be — no
  mechanism for this currently exists in Rule 17.1a, which (per 17.1a.4) models deploy/limber
  identically to an HMG/mortar regardless of tow vehicle, so this is flavor/context rather than
  an immediately actionable rule change.
- **Could not be manhandled off-road by its own crew, unlike the 37mm.** Patton's same quote:
  "the 57mm gun can't be moved by the crew off-road," a real, sourced, negative point of
  comparison against the lighter gun it replaced (weight ratio given elsewhere in Tank Archives
  as 1239 kg for the M1 vs 414 kg for the 37mm M3 — roughly triple). Relevant if this project's
  towed-AT-gun mechanic is ever extended to model off-road repositioning without a tow vehicle;
  no such mechanism currently exists in Rule 17.1a.
- **Real-world tow-vehicle shortages at the actual start of this project's window.** The 34th
  Infantry Division photograph cited by Tank Archives (4 July 1943, Rabat, Morocco, days before
  the Sicily landings) shows M1 guns being towed by improvised M3 half-tracks because the
  intended Dodge trucks had not yet reached the theater — a real, dated, minor logistics wrinkle
  at the very start of the gun's combat career, not something Rule 17.1a needs to model, but
  worth knowing if a scenario designer wants an early-Sicily flavor detail.
- **The gun-squad carried a bazooka as a secondary, close-range AT weapon**, per FM 23-75 (see
  Crew Size above) — i.e. even before the towed gun itself fires, the same 10-man unit had an
  independent short-range AT capability. Not modeled by Rule 17.1a as written (which represents
  the towed gun only), flagged only in case the designer wants this unit's counter to reflect
  a secondary close-defense capability the PaK40/6pdr crews are not documented as having.
- No source found this session suggests any unusually fast or slow emplacement/displacement
  time for the M1 relative to the HMG/mortar baseline Rule 7.6 (via 17.1a.4) already assumes —
  flagged as "nothing notable found," not "confirmed absent," matching the same honest-gap
  language both the PaK40 and 6pdr files already used for their own guns.

## Confidence Notes

- **Barrel length (L/50, matching the existing British curve's barrel):** well-established,
  directly stated in Wikipedia's wikitext with a specific Zaloga page citation. High confidence.
- **1943 ammunition was uncapped AP shot M70 only, not the capped M86/M85 shell:** well-
  established — two independent sources (Wikipedia/Zaloga and Tank Archives/translated Soviet
  testing data) agree on both the fact and its rough timing (capped shell arriving no earlier
  than "end of summer 1944" per Tank Archives). This is the load-bearing fact behind this file's
  "don't reuse the sixpdr curve as-is for a 1943 entry" conclusion — high confidence in the fact
  itself; the *design conclusion* drawn from it (that a separate curve is warranted) is this
  document's own reasoning by analogy to the PaK40's APCBC/APCR precedent, not something a
  source states directly.
- **M70 muzzle velocity (853 m/s / 2800 fps):** two independent sources agree exactly (Wikipedia
  wikitext table and Tank Archives text), both apparently drawing on the same underlying Zaloga
  2005 monograph — treat as well-corroborated but effectively single-ultimate-source.
- **M70 penetration table (135/112/89/70/55mm at 100/500/1000/1500/2000m):** single-sourced
  (Tank Archives only), and the obliquity convention ("at 90 degrees") was not independently
  confirmed to mean the same thing as this project's own 0°-obliquity convention already used
  for the 6pdr's Bird & Livingston table — flagged as a real open question, not assumed
  equivalent, before anyone tries to fit a curve from these numbers.
- **US ammunition designation M86 (Wikipedia) vs. M85 (Tank Archives):** a genuine,
  unresolved naming discrepancy between the two sources used this session for the same round.
- **Crew size:** genuinely three different figures (10 / informally-derived 6 / 5), the
  widest spread this project's towed-AT-gun research has produced so far — see Crew Size above
  for full detail on why none of the three was picked over the others.
- **9-gun Anti-tank Company (3 platoons of 3), replacing a 12-gun 37mm company, per the 15
  July / 26 May 1943 TO&E:** well-established, two independent sources agree on the structure
  and closely on the totals (57 vs. 54, reconciled above as a scope difference, not a real
  conflict).
- **Tow vehicle correction (1½-ton 6×6, not 3/4-ton):** well-established, two independent
  sources agree explicitly and specifically contrast it with the 37mm's own 3/4-ton tow truck.
- **Crew quality tier:** genuinely not found, and — unlike the 6pdr's Robaa Valley case — no
  action was found this session from which even an informal quality inference could be drawn.
- **Sicily-debut/Patton anecdote:** real and dated, but single-sourced this session (Tank
  Archives, itself citing Zaloga 2005) and not independently cross-checked against a second
  site the way the 6pdr's Robaa Valley anecdote was — moderate, not high, confidence.
- **WebSearch budget exhaustion:** this session's general-purpose web-search tool had already
  used its full session budget (200/200 calls) before this task began, elsewhere in the same
  conversation. Research here relied on direct `WebFetch`/`curl` retrieval of specific,
  individually-chosen URLs instead of iterative search-then-follow-links the way the PaK40 and
  6pdr files' own research passes worked. This plausibly narrowed the search, particularly for
