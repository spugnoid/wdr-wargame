# The Military Sniper Since 1914 — Martin Pegler (Osprey Elite 68)

## Sources

- Martin Pegler, *The Military Sniper Since 1914* (Osprey Elite 68, 2001),
  66pp. Image-only scan (`Creator: Image2PDF`, no text layer, confirmed via
  `pdfinfo`) — read in full via direct page-image inspection at 150dpi,
  all 66 pages, including the 12 color-plate pages and their commentary
  pages. This is the first pass against Section 20 (`section_20__snipers.rst`),
  which currently has **no sourcing file behind its stat rows at all** — a
  real, previously unflagged gap, not a re-check of a logged open question.
- Book scope note: it is a general history from the Boer War to the Gulf
  War, not a 1943-scoped TOE reference. Roughly 9 of 66 pages are WWII-specific
  narrative (pp.24-32); the rest is WWI, Korea/Vietnam/postwar narrative, or
  color-plate art. Cited page numbers below are the book's own printed page
  numbers (footer), not PDF file-index numbers.

## Findings

### 1. Ballistic/practical basis for the uniform fire line — a genuinely mixed answer, not a clean negative

The book does NOT support treating the five nations' rifle/scope combinations
as mechanically identical in a naive sense — real, sourced performance
differences exist — but it also independently arrives at the same
"convergence" conclusion this project's design already assumes, for a
different reason than the rule-guide currently states.

- **Real national differences the book documents:**
  - US Army combo (M1903A3/A4 + 2.5x Weaver M73B1, adopted 1942) is
    explicitly the weakest of the five: a commercial deer-hunting scope
    that "proved rather fragile when subjected to the rigours of field use
    in the Pacific," with no iron sights as backup (p.27).
  - US Marine Corps combo (issue '03 Springfield + Unertl scope, Corps
    sniping school opened Fort Bragg Dec 1942) is explicitly called out as
    *better* than the Army's own rifle: "a potent combination, providing
    target magnification out to ranges unattainable by the standard issue
    Weaver or Lyman scopes... in capable hands it was possible to shoot
    accurately to over 1,000 yards" (p.28) — see Finding 2 below; this is a
    within-nation split the current single "US sniper" row doesn't capture.
  - German K98k/G43 combo is called the most numerous and varied: "the
    German sniper had the greatest choice of telescope-equipped rifles of
    any nation... some ten standard models plus a host of variants" by
    1944 (p.27).
  - Soviet Moisin-Nagant quality is explicitly inconsistent: "the veteran
    Moisin could achieve good accuracy out to 800 metres, but as the war
    progressed quality varied greatly; some captured examples that were
    tested were reckoned to be of use only out to 400 yards or so" (p.26).
  - British No.4 Mk I(T) (adopted 1942, hand-rebuilt by Holland & Holland)
    is called out as "very popular and particularly accurate at longer
    ranges," with a real anecdote of hits at 700 yards in Italy (p.30).
- **But the book's own conclusion cuts the other way at the effective-range
  level that matters for this project's scale.** Even Matthias Hertzenauer —
  named as one of the most skilled German snipers of the war — is quoted
  reckoning a K98k with a 6x scope good for "400 metre head or 600 metre
  body hit," and the book immediately qualifies this: "it should be borne
  in mind that Hertzenauer was an extraordinarily gifted shot" (p.31). The
  book's general verdict on WWII-era service sniping rifles as a class:
  "in practice the use of ordinary production military rifles allied to the
  relatively low power of most telescopic sights meant that true long-range
  shooting — i.e. at ranges of over 600 yards — was rare," and a named
  British sniping officer's benchmark for "a superb shot" is "five shot
  2-inch groups at 100 yards" — which the book itself calls "really not
  much of an improvement over the performance of Great War rifles" (p.30).
- **Net read:** the *equipment* differed by nation in real, citable ways,
  but the book's own synthesis is that battlefield-effective performance
  across all five nations' bolt-action service rifles converged to roughly
  the same practical envelope (call it 400-600m, with fast falloff beyond
  it) — which is functionally consistent with (though not identical
  reasoning to) this project's existing uniform-fire-line rule-guide
  argument that "position, patience, and target selection" dominate over
  weapon differences at this scale. This is a genuine, moderate-confidence
  affirmation of the design choice, not a clean negative and not a reason
  to change any number.

### 2. National training-program quality/scale — strong material, and one real actionable gap

This is the strongest section of the book relative to this project's
existing Morale-tier assignments.

- **Soviet:** the book gives real scale AND a real quality caveat that maps
  almost exactly onto this project's existing veteran/regular split. Scale:
  "the Russians claimed that by 1938 six million soldiers had qualified for
  the 'Voroshiloff Sharpshooter' badge" and "over 53,000 Moisin-Nagant
  sniping rifles were manufactured up to 1938; by 1942 the same number were
  being manufactured *annually*" (p.25) — a mass program. Quality caveat,
  stated directly: "in practice, wartime Soviet snipers were probably less
  well trained than their counterparts in other armies... an experienced
  sniper would be allocated a couple of neophyte snipers who, if they were
  lucky, received two weeks' front-line training before they were left to
  fend for themselves" (p.25). A contemporary British sniping officer,
  Captain Charles Shore, is quoted as harshly skeptical of Soviet sniping
  claims ("not as we know it... sharpshooting in its lowest form," p.26-27),
  though the book itself calls this verdict "probably unjust." Named
  standouts (Vasily Zaitsev, 149 credited kills, p.23; Senior Sergeant Roza
  Shanina, 54 kills, Order of Glory, p.57) support a veteran tier for elite
  individuals atop a much larger, variably-trained regular base — this is
  close to direct confirmation of the existing Soviet veteran/regular split,
  not just consistency with it.
- **German:** confirms a hard reversal. A December 1931 German Chief of
  Army Command memo ordered worn-out sniper rifles "used up" and NOT
  replaced, judging sniping "tactically irrelevant" (p.24) — Germany
  started WWII with effectively no sniper program. Reversal came specifically
  because "the volume and effect of Soviet marksmanship seriously worried
  [the Germans], to the extent that they adopted a training programme for
  their own snipers based upon that used by the Russians" (p.26), driven
  under Himmler's Waffen-SS sniper program. By 1944, "there were no less
  than 30 German sniper training schools in existence" (p.27), producing
  named aces (Sepp Allenberger and Matthias Hertzenauer credited with over
  600 Russians between them, p.31) — solid support for the veteran tier.
- **British:** confirms neglect-then-rapid-recovery. Post-WWI, sniper
  complement was cut to 8 men per battalion and telescopic-sight stocks
  were broken up and sold off in 1921 (p.24). Recovery was fast once
  war came: a training school stood up at Bisley by 1940, with the Lovat
  Scouts (their prewar stalking/camouflage specialists) brought back in to
  teach, on an extended three-week course (p.29) — and combined
  Commonwealth counter-sniper teams in Burma inflicted 296 casualties for
  the loss of 2 of their own 48 snipers (p.29). Supports the veteran tier
  by the project's 1943 baseline, despite a rocky start.
- **US — the one genuinely actionable finding:** the book draws an explicit,
  sourced contrast this project's roster does not currently capture. US
  Army: "prewar US Army interest in sniping had been virtually
  non-existent, the training school at Camp Perry providing only a brief
  course with almost no practical field training" (p.27) — consistent with
  the current single "regular" tier assignment. US Marine Corps, by
  contrast: "the Marine Corps had always taken a very positive view of
  sniping and actively encouraged target shooting... trained in scouting
  and sniping on an informal basis until December 1942, when a Corps
  sniping school was opened at Fort Bragg" (p.28), paired with the
  better-performing Unertl-scoped '03 Springfield noted in Finding 1. This
  project's current roster has only one "US sniper — regular" row (using
  the Army's specific rifle/scope combo, the M1903A4 + Weaver, as its
  flavor text) with no separate Marine Corps row. The book's own material
  would support a US Marine Corps sniper counter at the veteran tier as a
  distinct addition — **not applied here**, flagged for the coordinator
  below.
- **Japanese — a real internal tension in the book, flagged not resolved:**
  the main WWII narrative describes Japanese snipers under Col Namio
  Tatsumi's guidance as skilled, camouflage-expert marksmen fielding real
  4x/2.5x-scoped Type 97/99 rifles (p.28-29) — consistent with the existing
  veteran tier. But the plate commentary for a Japanese Special Naval
  Landing Force sniper figure states directly: "although not highly trained
  snipers — they were taught to shoot only to moderate ranges — the
  Japanese were masters of camouflage and suicidally brave, often courting
  certain death to ensure that they hit their chosen target" (p.56). The
  book itself doesn't reconcile these — Army/Tatsumi-trained marksmen vs.
  SNLF naval-infantry riflemen using sniper tactics without matching
  marksmanship training appear to be two different populations the book
  folds under one "Japanese sniper" umbrella. Doesn't contradict the
  existing veteran-tier assignment (the doctrine/camouflage/suicidal
  determination case for a high Morale value stands on its own), but it's
  a genuine ambiguity in the source, not something this pass resolved.

### 3. AMO base-4 assumption — clean negative on the specific figure, one soft supporting data point

No rounds-per-successful-engagement or ammunition-discipline doctrine
figure of the kind Rule 20.1.3 would need was found anywhere in the book.
This is a clean negative for a citable numeric basis for AMO = 4.

One soft, thematically-related data point: a German plate commentary
describes a Scharfschütze operating from a fixed, camouflaged hide who
"will probably fire only four or five careful shots in a day" (p.56) —
same order of magnitude as this project's AMO 4 base, but describes a
whole day of a static ambush position, not "shots available before running
dry in a scenario," so it is not a citable basis for the figure, only a
loose plausibility check. Separately, a July 1944 German logistics report
(quoted in full, p.31) treats sharpshooter-grade ammunition as a real,
tracked, scarce resource distinct from standard ball ammunition ("the
General of Infantry... demands the production of suitable sharpshooter
ammunition at a volume of 20 million cartridges monthly") — this supports
the general design philosophy of tracking sniper ammunition as scarce
(consistent with reusing the mortar AMO system, Rule 20.1.3), but gives no
figure usable for the AMO value itself, and it's Germany-specific: the
book states Britain and the US used ordinary quality-controlled ball
ammunition for snipers, not a specially manufactured sniper-only product.

### 4. A dated combat anecdote — several candidates, one specifically 1943

- **Best match to the project's 1943 baseline:** First Sergeant Fusco, US
  27th Infantry Division, fighting on Makin Island in **late 1943**,
  quoted directly on dealing with Japanese tree snipers: "Smoking out the
  snipers that were in the trees was the worst part of it. We couldn't spot
  them even with glasses and it made our advance very slow... if one of our
  men began to fire rapidly into a tree or ground location, we knew that he
  had spotted a sniper, and those who could see took up the fire. When we
  saw no enemy we fired occasional shots into trees that looked likely"
  (p.29). Named individual, named unit, exact place and year, direct
  quotation — a strong, low-risk citation, and thematically apt for
  Rule 20.6 (counter-sniper procedures via area fire on a suspected
  position rather than precise detection).
- **Higher color, later date (not 1943):** Corporal Arthur Hare, a British
  prewar Regular sniper serving in the advance through Belgium, Holland,
  and Germany in 1944-45, is quoted describing a single day's action —
  shooting down a sentry and two officers/messengers at a bridge, with his
  three-man sniper team accounting for roughly 200 German dead, wounded,
  and captured over ten minutes of action during a river-crossing assault —
  for which he was awarded the Military Medal (pp.32-33). Directly named,
  dated to the NW Europe campaign, but not 1943 specifically and the exact
  date/location within 1944-45 isn't given by the book.
- **Explicitly flagged as low-confidence by the book itself:** an unnamed
  Soviet sniper on the Eastern Front survived for nearly a week inside a
  knocked-out T-34, living among its dead crew, picking off a resting
  German Panzer unit over five days before being spotted by the vapor of
  his own breath in cold sunlight — "there is no record of his fate" (p.26).
  Genuinely evocative, but no name, unit, or exact date beyond "one German
  account" from the 1941 invasion period — the book's own hedge language
  ("one German account") is carried forward here rather than rounded off.
  The same page gives a real dated/named unit figure worth noting instead:
  the German 465th Infantry Regiment lost 75 dead and 25 missing in a few
  hours to "tree snipers" during a September 1941 assault on a wooded area
  (p.26) — dated and unit-attributed, though also 1941 not 1943.

### 5. Other findings relevant to Section 20 mechanics

- **Shoot-and-scoot is real doctrine, but not universal** — Rule 20.4's
  optional reposition (20.4.4 allows staying put) is actually well
  supported by a tension in the book: some snipers moved immediately after
  firing, but a German Scharfschütze plate commentary describes snipers
  deliberately building elaborate fixed camouflaged hides (a canvas "dead
  horse" built on a metal frame) specifically *to avoid needing to move*,
  firing only a handful of aimed shots per day from the same spot (p.56).
  This maps cleanly onto the existing Rule 20.4.4 (reposition is optional)
  rather than contradicting anything.
- **Patient, multi-turn stalking of a single target is directly attested**:
  US Marine Sgt Ed Kugler's Vietnam account of a six-shot, two-hour
  engagement against one distant target (p.48) is post-WWII but reinforces
  the F1 "one careful shot" pacing already in Rule 20.1.2 as a
  cross-era constant of the sniper's rate of fire, not a WWII-specific
  data point.
- **No equivalent to the CONTACT-marker/area-suppression abstraction is
  described mechanically** (expected — it's a game abstraction), but the
  book's own text is full of the phenomenon it's modeling: psychological
  effect on units who merely suspect a sniper is present, on both the
  Eastern Front and in Vietnam ("nobody much cared to enquire too deeply
  what a sniper was doing, or why," p.48, of the license a sniping rifle
  granted). Nothing here calls the mechanic into question or needs to be
  cited into the rule text; the existing Rule 20.5.4 rationale already
  covers this without needing this book as a specific citation.

## Confidence Notes

- Image-scan-only, no text layer — read via direct page-image inspection,
  all 66 pages, not sampled. High confidence in the transcriptions above;
  quoted passages are as printed.
- This is a general narrative history spanning 1914-present, not a 1943-scoped
  TOE or ballistics reference — only ~9 pages are WWII-specific narrative.
  That said, unlike the Encyclopedia of Weapons of WWII (E.145) or Deutsche
  Panzer (E.146), this book's genre (a unit/training/doctrine history) is
  exactly the right kind of source for Section 20's Morale/quality questions,
  even though it is the wrong kind of source for the AMO/ballistics
  questions — a split verdict by question, not a single "wrong genre"
  verdict for the whole book.
- The Q1 finding is explicitly non-binary: real per-nation rifle/scope
  differences are documented, but the book's own effective-range synthesis
  supports (for different stated reasons) the same practical convergence
  this project's uniform fire line already assumes. This is reported
  faithfully as a "moderate affirmation," not inflated into a clean
  confirmation.
- The Japanese SNLF-vs-Army-trained-marksman tension (Finding 2) is a real
  ambiguity in the source itself, in the same spirit as the US Combat
  Engineer file's flagged "six vs. eight specialties" inconsistency
  (`us_combat_engineer_1943.md`) — noted, not resolved, not forced into a
  single verdict.

## Open Questions / Gaps for Follow-up

1. **RESOLVED, design note E.150.** US Marine Corps sniper added as a
   distinct roster row — "US sniper (Marine Corps) — veteran" alongside
   the relabeled "US sniper (Army) — regular" — citing the Corps's
   dedicated sniping school (Dec 1942) and its better-performing
   Unertl-scoped '03 Springfield. Fire line deliberately left identical
   to every other row (see Finding 1: the book's own effective-range
   synthesis doesn't support a fire-line distinction), so the gap is
   expressed through Morale only, consistent with every other national
   quality difference in this table.
2. **AMO base of 4** remains entirely uncited by any source this project
   has read to date, this book included — still an open question needing
   either a primary-source doctrine manual or a different reference genre
   entirely (the project's standing pattern from E.145/E.146 suggests
   general histories and photo compendiums are unlikely to help; a
   training-manual-style primary source is a more promising lead).
3. **The Japanese SNLF/Army-marksman quality tension** (Finding 2) is left
   exactly as ambiguous as the book leaves it — a future pass with a
   Japan-specific source (parallel to `japan_engineers_1943.md`'s approach
   for engineers) could try to disentangle whether this project's single
   "Japanese sniper — veteran" row is best understood as Army-trained
   marksmen specifically, with SNLF/naval-infantry snipers perhaps meriting
   their own, lower-quality row — not attempted here.
4. No new anecdote was applied to the rule text or design notes in this
   pass; the First Sergeant Fusco (Makin Island, late 1943) quotation is
   flagged as the strongest 1943-dated candidate if the coordinator wants
   a flavor-text or design-note anecdote for Rule 20.6 (counter-sniper
   procedures) in a future pass.
