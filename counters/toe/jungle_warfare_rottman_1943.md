# World War II Jungle Warfare Tactics — Dr Stephen Bull (Osprey Elite 151)

## Sources

- Dr Stephen Bull, *World War II Jungle Warfare Tactics* (Osprey Elite 151,
  2007), 64pp of content plus 8 unnumbered color-plate pages (A-H) and a
  2pp index. Image-only scan (`Creator: Adobe Acrobat 8.1 Image Conversion
  Plug-in`, confirmed via `pdfinfo`, no text layer) — read in full via
  direct page-image inspection at the Read tool's default resolution, all
  67 PDF pages (cover, contents, title, copyright/notes, all 64 numbered
  content pages, all 8 plate pages, the index, and the back cover), in
  four batches. This is the first pass checking this project's terrain
  system (Section 4, Appendix B) and Rule 20.7's Japanese sniper row
  against a book specifically about Pacific/jungle-theater tactics, rather
  than a general history or a European-theater-focused reference.
- Book scope note: covers British Empire, Australian, US, and Japanese
  jungle tactics across the whole 1941-45 Pacific/SE Asia war, organized
  chronologically (Allied failure 1941-42, Japanese success 1941-42,
  Allied reaction 1942-43, Long Range Penetration/Chindits 1943-44, FM
  72-20 US tactics 1944, "To the Last Man" 1944-45). It is a tactics-and-
  doctrine narrative, not a TOE/organization reference or a ballistics/
  cover-value reference — closer in genre to the sniper book (E.149) than
  to the fortifications book (E.151) in what kind of numbers it does and
  doesn't supply. Cited page numbers are the book's own printed page
  footers; plate pages are cited by their printed plate letter (A-H).

## Findings

### 1. Does the book support a distinct Jungle terrain type, or does Dense Woods cover it adequately? — a real, hedged case for "harder," and a real case that jungle isn't one thing

The book gives no single number convertible into a Cover/Intervening/MP
figure the way this project's tables need, but it gives substantial
comparative and qualitative material, most of which points toward jungle
being **at least as severe as, and arguably harsher than,** this
project's existing Dense Woods (Cover +4, -3/hex intervening, MP 3),
alongside a genuine complication: the book insists jungle is not one
terrain type at all.

- **Visibility is described as more extreme than ordinary dense
  woodland:** "While sounds and smells seem amplified, visibility is
  severely limited — sometimes little more than arm's length" (p.3).
  Rule 4.4.3 currently requires three or more *consecutive* hexes of
  Dense woods to block LOS outright; this book's own arm's-length figure
  is a real, if qualitative, data point that jungle's worst stretches
  degrade sight distance more severely than the "hard to see through, not
  impossible" standard that threshold implies.
- **Intervening-fire degradation reads as real and severe:** "Long-range
  machine gun fire is not practised in jungle. The guns are usually sited
  for cross fire" (p.20, on Japanese MG defensive siting). A Vickers
  medium machine gun's rated 3,000-yard effective range was "rarely
  achievable due to the lack of fields of observation and fire in
  forested terrain" (p.59, Plate A1 commentary). Japanese defenders were
  doctrinally cautioned not to open fire until an enemy was "about 50
  yards, or just outside grenade range" (p.55) — even automatic weapons
  held fire that long. This is a real, repeated pattern across three
  independent passages (British MMG doctrine, Japanese MG siting doctrine,
  Japanese general defensive doctrine) all describing engagement ranges
  far shorter than what Dense Woods' -3/hex penalty implicitly assumes is
  still a meaningful firing distance — a genuine, moderate-confidence case
  that jungle's intervening penalty, if it were its own terrain type,
  should plausibly be harsher than Dense Woods' -3/hex, not identical to it.
- **Movement is described as extremely slow for one kind of jungle, and
  not especially slow for another — the book's own point, not this file's
  interpretation:** a Japanese "chopping group" (troops cutting a fresh
  path) "was expected to maintain a speed of just a quarter of a mile per
  hour, with a maximum distance of four to six miles per day being
  achieved in good conditions" (p.4); the Japanese 42nd Infantry Regiment
  managed "just three miles in three days" through "the 'inferno of heat'
  in the jungle at Kampar, Malaya" (p.4-5); US training literature stated
  a trained man could clear "100 square yards of trail in five hours"
  (p.5). These are all citable, real, order-of-magnitude figures — but
  they describe **cutting a new path through thick "secondary" or "low"
  jungle** specifically (bamboo, thorn thickets, undergrowth, p.4), not
  jungle in general.
- **The book explicitly denies jungle is monolithic, which cuts against a
  single new terrain type more than it supports one:** "only 'primary'
  jungle — known in Australia as 'high' jungle, with its mature trees and
  lofty canopy — is truly natural," with an open floor of "dead leaves and
  seedling trees" and "practically no earth visible and certainly no
  grass or flowers" (p.3, quoting Col Spencer Chapman) — and "this genuine
  jungle of half-light and deep shadows can sometimes be crossed without
  too much difficulty" (p.3-4). By contrast, cleared/regrown "secondary"
  or "low" jungle is the kind that "any path has to be cut laboriously
  through" (p.4). A third form is mangrove swamp and tall grass, and
  paddy fields are a fourth distinct obstacle entirely. "Few jungles were
  genuinely 'impenetrable', in fact, and it is a myth that they were
  believed to be so in 1941" (p.4). This is the book's own repeated
  thesis, not an incidental aside — it directly complicates any single
  "Jungle" terrain-type row, since the book describes at least two
  meaningfully different densities (open-floor primary jungle, thick-cut
  secondary jungle) with very different real movement implications, plus
  swamp/paddy as separate obstacles again.
- **No comparative bullet-stopping/cover data was found at all** — unlike
  the fortifications book's "safe thickness of material" table
  (E.151/`us_field_fortifications_osprey029_1943.md`), this book never
  discusses jungle vegetation's protective value against fire in
  quantified or even clearly comparative terms relative to temperate
  woodland. Its material is entirely about visibility, movement, and
  engagement range — a real gap in what it can support for the Cover
  column specifically, as distinct from the Intervening-penalty column.
- **One existing mechanic is directly, independently affirmed rather than
  challenged:** the book's own description of Japanese MG siting — "sited
  in ravines to deny this route of approach... and on reverse slopes to
  catch troops as they come over the crest" (p.20) — maps cleanly onto
  this project's existing Reverse Slope mechanic (Rule 4.4.4) with no
  change needed; jungle scenarios clearly benefit from that rule already
  existing, rather than needing a jungle-specific replacement for it.
- **Net read:** the book supports a real, moderate-confidence argument
  that jungle's intervening-fire penalty and LOS-block threshold could
  plausibly be harsher than Dense Woods if a dedicated terrain type were
  built, and that at least the "secondary/low jungle" variant plausibly
  costs more than 3 MP to move through — but it does **not** support
  folding "jungle" into one new row cleanly, since the book's central,
  repeated point is that jungle terrain varies enormously by type. See
  the explicitly-flagged proposal in the Open Questions section below;
  nothing here was applied to Appendix B, Section 4, or Rule 7.2.

### 2. Japanese sniper tactics — a real texture addition, not a resolution of the Army/SNLF tension

`military_sniper_pegler_1943.md` (E.149) flagged an unresolved tension
between Army-trained marksmen under Col Namio Tatsumi's guidance
(scoped Type 97/99 rifles, real marksmanship training) and Special Naval
Landing Force troops using "sniper tactics" (tree positions) without
matching training. This book was read as a plausible candidate to
disentangle that tension, being specifically about Pacific/jungle
tactics. It does not resolve it.

- This book never mentions Col Tatsumi, a dedicated Japanese Army
  marksmanship program, or scoped rifles at all. Its only treatment of
  "sniper" as a Japanese tactic is doctrinal and organizational, not
  personnel/training-based: "The Japanese habit was to establish an
  outpost line consisting mainly of snipers and observers, who would act
  as a tripwire to warn the main position. When overrun, the outpost line
  could fall back to the main defence; but equally, individual snipers
  could go to ground, opening fire again into the rear of the attack,
  usually at the cost of their lives. Locating a sniper could be
  extremely difficult, even after he had fired" (pp.54-55).
- This description is closer in spirit to the SNLF/naval-infantry side of
  Pegler's tension (ordinary infantry posted in trees or spider holes as
  part of standard defensive doctrine, not a distinct marksman corps) than
  to the Army/Tatsumi side — but it describes this as standard practice
  for **Japanese infantry positions generally** across the whole Pacific
  theater, not as an SNLF-specific practice distinct from an Army
  practice. The book simply doesn't organize its material by "which
  Japanese sniper population is this," so it neither confirms nor denies
  that a separately-trained Army marksman corps existed alongside this
  general tree-position tactic.
- Directly relevant supporting material for Rule 20.5/20.6's existing
  counter-sniper framing (not the Army/SNLF question, but a genuine
  affirmation of adjacent mechanics): "Advancing troops were to accept
  snipers as a given; 'when sniped, dive for cover. Then move quickly to
  a new position, locate the sniper and kill him'. If caught alone and
  outnumbered, men were to remain hidden and motionless. Holding fire was
  a recognized tactic" (p.52). This is consistent with, and adds no new
  information beyond, what E.149 already found in Pegler.
- **Net read:** a clean negative on resolving Finding 2's Army/SNLF
  tension specifically — this book is the wrong kind of source for that
  question (it describes Japanese defensive doctrine broadly, not
  sniper-specific training organization), even though its subject matter
  is exactly right for the terrain questions above. Left exactly as
  ambiguous as Pegler left it; not forced toward either reading.

### 3. Daylight disorientation/movement-risk — real texture, but no clean anecdote of a Rule 7.7-shaped daytime "got lost" gap

Rule 7.7 (Night Movement Risk) and Rule 24.4.3 (its Fog extension) are
currently the only triggers for this project's "unit stops short of its
destination, disoriented" mechanic. The brief asked whether this book
documents an equivalent daylight risk in jungle terrain specifically.

- Real, repeated material describes jungle navigation as a constant,
  general hazard, not framed as night-specific: "jungle navigation is
  rarely easy: as Maj Fergusson explained, one is 'submerged' in the
  jungle, [and] to reach a hill with a view is like suddenly emerging
  from a deck hatch on a boat... The problem is not merely lack of
  landmarks" (p.5). Pre-war British doctrine (*Infantry Section Leading*,
  1938) held that maintaining direction in jungle "required 'constant
  attention'," while "'men experienced in wood and bush craft develop an
  instinct for keeping direction'" (p.9) — both framed as a general
  jungle-terrain problem, not a nighttime-specific one.
- The clearest *anecdote* of a unit-level disorientation gap found in this
  book — "During the night a large gap had appeared in one of the
  columns... the King's Own managed not only to extricate themselves but
  also to inflict more casualties than they received" (p.48) — is
  explicitly a **night** action, already inside Rule 7.7's existing
  trigger condition, so it does not demonstrate a new daylight-specific
  gap this project's rules don't cover.
- **Net read:** the "jungle navigation is genuinely hard, all the time"
  texture is real and citable, and is a fair basis for a future
  coordinator conversation about whether jungle terrain should extend
  Rule 7.7's trigger beyond Night/Fog — but this book does not supply the
  kind of concrete, worked daylight-specific anecdote (a named unit
  getting lost and arriving somewhere wrong in daylight) that would make
  a strong citation for that specific extension. Reported as a moderate,
  not strong, finding — a real gap the book gestures at rather than
  proves with a worked example.

### 4. Ambush/close-range combat emphasis — strong, repeated, quantified support for extreme-close-range jungle engagement

This is the strongest, most directly game-relevant finding in the book.

- **"50 yards was regarded as a long shot in the jungle"** (p.52) — a
  direct, quotable, general statement about jungle engagement range.
- **Japanese defensive fire doctrine, independently corroborating the
  same figure:** "optimum range was thought to be about 50 yards, or just
  outside grenade range... Automatic weapons would remain silent until
  Allied troops entered their lanes of fire and then 'annihilate the
  enemy before he enters the position. A heavy volume of fire is
  delivered at close range'" (p.55).
- **A named weapon's rated range explicitly contrasted with jungle
  practice:** the Vickers medium machine gun's "effective range of 3,000
  yards was rarely achievable due to the lack of fields of observation
  and fire in forested terrain" (p.59, Plate A1).
- **Ambush doctrine across all combatants treats close range as the
  norm, not the exception:** "In jungle country the reduced visibility
  and the relative difficulty of support fire weapons made ambushes a
  critical element of infantry tactics" (p.63, Plate H introduction).
  Real photographed/captioned examples describe engagement at 60 yards
  (a US .30-cal MG team at Mount Tambu, New Guinea, 1943, captioned "the
  enemy are no more than 60 yards away," p.27) and point-blank range
  (Japanese ambush tactics allowing vehicles or troops to close before
  firing, p.19).
- **Net read:** this is a genuine, low-risk, well-corroborated (multiple
  independent passages, different nations, different weapon types all
  landing in the same 50-60 yard band) piece of context directly useful
  for Rule 8.9 (Adjacent Fire Bonus, +2 eFP at range 1, +3 at range 0) and
  Rule 8.7 (Long Range Cap) — not as a reason to change either rule's
  numbers (this project's map scale and hex-range abstraction aren't
  addressed by the book at all), but as strong flavor-text/design-note
  material establishing that jungle combat's real-world character
  matches this project's existing close-range-favoring mechanics rather
  than requiring new ones.

### 5. A dated, named 1943 combat anecdote — two real candidates

- **Best match, directly quotable and precisely dated:** Major Bernard
  Fergusson's Chindit column during **Operation Longcloth (February-April
  1943)**, recounting the Bonchaung ambush from a wounded survivor's
  account: "They had walked head on into a lorry load of Japanese
  standing in the village... They thought they had killed everybody, for
  the loss of two killed and Bill Edge and one or two others wounded...
  John was waiting only to collect his platoon, when suddenly a new
  machine gun had opened up, and hit him and several more. While he was
  telling me this, there was a sudden report just beside my ear, and I
  spun round to find Peter Dorans with a smoking rifle, and one of the
  'dead' Japs in the road writhing" (p.45). Named individuals (Bill Edge,
  Peter Dorans), a named operation, and an exact date range within 1943 —
  a strong, low-risk citation.
- **Second candidate, dated but less textually detailed:** a photo caption
  describing "Australian soldiers in close combat with Japanese troops,
  fighting from the huge foxhole under the buttress roots of a tree at
  Mount Tambu, New Guinea, in **August 1943**. While one man keeps the
  enemy pinned with short bursts from his Thompson SMG, his mate readies
  a No.36 grenade. In jungle conditions, especially at night, the hand
  grenade was a weapon of great value" (p.25) — dated and located
  precisely, but without named individuals or unit designation beyond
  the general Australian force in New Guinea.
- **Not 1943, but notable:** LtCol Leonard's account of a jungle tree
  ambush in Burma, **February 1942** — vivid and precisely dated, but a
  year outside this project's 1943 baseline (p.19-20).

### 6. Other findings relevant to Section 4/20/22

- **Section 22 (Scenario Design Guidelines) — a real, citable doctrinal
  basis for "no linear front line" jungle scenario guidance, matching
  this project's own noted gap:** British 1942 doctrine stated plainly,
  "'In considering security in overgrown country it must be constantly
  borne in mind that there will seldom, if ever, be a time when a
  definite line will separate the area under friendly control from that
  in the hands of the enemy. There will be no standard front with its
  area of "no man's land"'" (p.24). This is a strong, quotable basis for
  a future Section 22 addition specifically calling out Pacific/jungle
  scenarios as favoring all-round "defensive islands" rather than a
  contiguous front — not designed or added here, since Section 22 changes
  are out of this pass's scope, but flagged as a strong lead.
- **Real, quantified Pacific-theater unit-spacing figures**, potentially
  useful for future Section 22 guidance on jungle scenario setup: the
  Australian 6th Division's 1943 training instructions specify a platoon
  "automatic defence" position "not to exceed 150 yards" in diameter, and
  company-level schemes "up to 300 yards across, allowing platoons to be
  up to 100 yards apart, and sections up to 50 yards" (p.26, diagram
  captions).
- **Tank/infantry cooperation in jungle** (tanks ranging up to 800 yards
  ahead of infantry on Pacific islands by 1944-45, pp.30-32, 41) is real
  and citable but outside this pass's Section 4/20/22 scope — noted only
  in passing, not pursued.
- **26th Indian ('Tiger Head') Division's 1944 Burma casualty
  breakdown** — 2,255 battle casualties, roughly 93% infantry, 3%
  artillery, under 1% engineers, 3% distributed among medics/signallers/
  HQ/RIASC (p.5) — a real, if not-1943, data point consistent with this
  project's general design assumption that infantry does most of the
  fighting and dying in jungle/close terrain, not directly actionable for
  any specific rule.

## Confidence Notes

- Image-scan-only, no text layer (confirmed via `pdfinfo`'s Creator
  field) — read via direct page-image inspection, all 67 PDF pages, not
  sampled. High confidence in the transcriptions and page attributions
  above; quoted passages are as printed, and page numbers are the book's
  own printed footers.
- This book is a strong genre match for the terrain/tactics/scenario-
  design questions (a tactics-and-doctrine narrative is exactly the right
  kind of source for "how did real jungle combat actually feel/behave"),
  but — like the fortifications book (E.151) — it does not supply a
  single number directly convertible into this project's abstract
  Cover/Intervening-penalty/MP-cost point scale. Findings on those
  questions are reported as qualitative and comparative, not as precise
  numeric confirmations, in the same spirit as E.151's "safe thickness of
  material" finding.
- Finding 1 is deliberately reported as double-edged: real evidence
  points toward jungle being *harsher* than Dense Woods in several
  respects, but the book's own repeated insistence that "jungle" covers
  several genuinely different terrain densities (primary/open-floor vs.
  secondary/thick-cut vs. swamp/paddy) is reported faithfully as a
  complication for any single new terrain type, not smoothed over to make
  a cleaner recommendation.
- Finding 2 is a genuine clean negative on the specific Army/SNLF
  question this pass set out to check, reported honestly rather than
  stretched to claim resolution — this book's material is organized by
  Japanese defensive doctrine in general, not by which population of
  snipers is being described.
- Finding 5's two 1943 candidates are reported with their real limits
  intact (Fergusson's account is a secondhand quotation of a wounded
  survivor's story; the Mount Tambu photo caption has no named
  individuals) rather than rounded up into stronger claims than the book
  supports.

## Open Questions / Gaps for Follow-up

1. **PROPOSAL FOR COORDINATOR REVIEW — NOT APPLIED.** Whether to add a
   dedicated Jungle terrain type to Appendix B / Section 4 (and Rule
   7.2's movement table) is a genuinely larger design decision than this
   documentation pass should make unilaterally, per this pass's own
   brief. If the coordinator wants to pursue it, this file's Finding 1
   supports (not proves) roughly the following, clearly labeled as
   extrapolation from qualitative evidence, not a citable number:
   - **Option A (single "Jungle" terrain type):** Cover +4 (unchanged
     from Dense Woods — no bullet-stopping/protection data was found in
     this book at all, so no basis exists to move this number either
     way); Intervening penalty -4/hex (one step harsher than Dense
     Woods' -3, based on the repeated real-world pattern of engagement
     ranges collapsing to ~50-60 yards even for automatic weapons and
     machine guns rated for far longer ranges); MP cost 4 (one step
     harsher than Dense Woods' 3, based on the "quarter mile per hour"
     chopping-group pace and "100 square yards of trail in five hours"
     figures); and consider lowering the full-LOS-block threshold
     (Rule 4.4.3) from 3 consecutive hexes to 2 for this terrain
     specifically, given the "little more than arm's length" visibility
     figure (p.3).
   - **Option B (two terrain types, more faithful to the book's own
     distinction):** a "Primary/High Jungle" type — walkable underfoot,
     closer to existing Light Woods for movement, but with Dense-Woods-
     or-harsher cover/intervening values reflecting the closed canopy's
     effect on sightlines and observation — and a separate "Secondary/
     Low Jungle" type, harsher than Dense Woods on all three axes,
     reflecting the "any path has to be cut" undergrowth the book
     describes. This option is more consistent with the book's repeated
     insistence that jungle isn't monolithic, at the cost of adding two
     new rows instead of one.
   - Either option leaves Section 22's zero Pacific-specific scenario
     guidance untouched; a future pass could pair a new terrain type with
     Section 22 guidance drawing on Finding 6's "no linear front line"
     and unit-spacing material.
   - **Not applied, not recommended over the status quo, and not ruled
     out** — this file's own evidence is real but comparative/qualitative
     rather than a precise citable figure, the same caveat E.151 applied
     to the fortifications book's protection data.
2. **The Japanese Army/SNLF sniper tension (from E.149) remains
   unresolved.** This book was a plausible candidate and came back a
   clean negative on that specific question, as detailed in Finding 2. A
   future pass would need a source organized around Japanese sniper
   *training and personnel* specifically (parallel to how
   `japan_engineers_1943.md` approached engineers) rather than general
   jungle tactics doctrine — not attempted here.
3. **Rule 7.7's Night/Fog-only scope for the "got lost" movement-risk
   check** is left exactly as-is. Finding 3 supports a real, general
   jungle-navigation-difficulty texture but not a strong daylight-
   specific anecdote; a future pass could look for a training-manual or
   unit-history source describing a specific daylight disorientation
   incident before proposing to extend Rule 7.7 to jungle terrain
   generally.
4. **Section 22 (Scenario Design Guidelines)'s zero Pacific-specific
   content** remains open. Finding 6 flags real, citable material (the
   "no linear front" doctrine, p.24; real Australian unit-spacing
   figures, p.26) that a future pass could use to add Pacific/jungle-
   specific scenario guidance — not designed or added here, as Section 22
   changes are out of this pass's scope.
5. No changes were made to `units.csv`, Appendix B, Rule 7.2, or any
   numeric value in Section 4 or Section 20 as part of this pass — see
   the corresponding design note (E.152) for what was and was not applied
   to the rule text.
